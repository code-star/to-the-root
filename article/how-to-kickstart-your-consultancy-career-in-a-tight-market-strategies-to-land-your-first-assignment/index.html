How to kickstart your consultancy career in a tight market: Strategies to land your first assignment

by
Maxwell Otto

The Dutch labor market is tight, which is characterized by low employment rates and scarcity of talent (CBS). In theory, this would simplify the search for assignments. However, the reality within consultancy firms is more nuanced. The domestic economy is growing (EU), though global macroeconomic instability has led to caution among private and public sector organizations.

The current global uncertainty is fueled by multiple factors. Ongoing geopolitical conflicts in Eastern Europe and the Middle East are disrupting supply chains. Additionally, the trade restrictions initiated by the United States of America has triggered a global slowdown on export investments (IMF). The postponement of investment decisions reduces the reliance of organizations on consultants. Less consulting opportunities appear on the market, which results in higher inactivity of consultants and prolonged bench times. This combination of factors is making it significantly more complex - especially for those at the start of their career - to land a new assignment.

The goal of this white paper is to offer data-driven insights, a real-world example, and practical strategies for (starting) IT consultants and managers. By combining market analyses with personal experiences, this paper provides tools to improve one’s positioning in the consultancy market.

The reality of a starting IT consultant

Many IT professionals face a sober reality when searching for a consulting assignment. For young professionals, the reality is worsening. There is a growing mismatch between (technical) skill demands, formal education, and industry certifications in the job market. This chapter explores how the mismatch negatively contributes to the reality of starting IT consultants.

The experience mismatch

Universities and vocational programs offer broad theoretical foundations, while industry certifications provide more specific knowledge and skills in their fields. However, clients rarely look for mere theoretical qualifications. They prioritize demonstrable, practical experience in equal or similar environments. This expectation creates a structural imbalance: ‘Experience is required to get hired, but an assignment is necessary to gain experience.’ This is widely known as the infamous ‘Catch-22’ paradox (Heller, 1961) for starting consultants. To counter this phenomenon, new consultants may train on-the-job, though few clients are willing to pay premium rates (even at junior level) for consultants.

The expectation mismatch

Rapid technological innovations have radically transformed the IT landscape. Emerging fields such as artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), cloud-native development and cybersecurity are currently dominating corporate agendas (McKinsey; Gartner). Because of the rise in digital maturity benchmarks, the baseline expectations for consultants have increased. Currently, clients expect both technical expertise and hands-on experience with the latest tools, frameworks, and industry best practices.

This technological evolution shortens the life cycle of skills and raises the entry level for IT professionals. In this rapidly changing IT world, current junior consultants may not meet the clients’ expectations anymore in the next few years.

The market mismatch

The reduced dependency on external labor force and macroeconomic uncertainty builds a challenging environment for IT consultants. Organizations publicly voice concerns about talent shortages (Shirer, 2024; Lipowski, 2024), though their procurement processes and assignment criteria increasingly filter out talented candidates who lack consulting experience or niche technical skills. This is partly caused by the dynamic technical landscape and the corresponding difficulty of defining the necessary skills (Sinha, 2025), which creates another Catch-22 paradox: ‘There is a high demand for IT expertise, yet entry-level consultants experience difficulties securing assignments due to inflated expectations and limited pathways to gain relevant experience.’

Experiences from the field

Theoretical models are suitable for highlighting structural mismatches in the market, though practical insights may appear from the eyes of a newcomer to the field. Each established consultant has faced their own difficulties before gaining the necessary experience to live up to the clients’ expectations. This chapter illustrates one variation of a junior IT consultant with an unrelated background and reflects several strategies to overcome entry-level barriers.

Traditional application approaches

As a starting IT consultant, one may experience that common strategies (e.g. applying via internal portals, submitting CVs) are ineffective. The main cause lays within the lack of work experience and the increased expectations from the clients. The traditional mechanisms of consulting firms tend to favor employees with a track record of billable projects. This leaves little room for eager junior consultants without relevant assignments that stand out. Especially in a tight market, we often see junior consultants lose out to experienced consultants.

During interviews, however, subjective characteristics may outweigh the résumé. As mentioned in Rivera (2012), candidates with similar life experiences, leisure activities and self-presentation styles are often favored by the hiring managers. Also, professionals (rather than HR representatives) look for the potential to work closely with the hired candidates. Work activities in consulting require a great deal of teamwork and client contact, therefore candidates who possess such qualities have a favorable position. However, this does not provide the foundation for finding an assignment for a client. Therefore, other strategies are needed to help junior consultants get the desired work experience.

Alternative application strategies

While attempting to find an assignment by adopting the traditional strategies, it is helpful to exploit alternative approaches. The social capital theory postulates that a candidate’s social network (e.g. contacts in other functions and organizational levels) is positively related to the candidate’s career success (Seibert, Kraimer & Liden, 2001). This theory shows the importance of visibility, relationships and profiling.

By being visible to managers and sales representatives, a candidate proactively contributes to the likelihood of being considered for upcoming projects. However, only physical presence does not change anything. Managers may be unaware of a candidate’s availability unless they are made aware of it. This underlines the importance of building relationships at the location. It is advisable to have work-related conversations, participate in social/business events, proactively try to help co-workers, and so on. Helping co-workers also has a profiling advantage, because it makes the candidate’s unique skills known to others.

Profiling helps to clarify the consultant’s preferences, so that opportunities may fit better for the candidate. Especially for junior consultants, who lack specialized skills and experience, it is advantageous to proclaim any existing preference. If not, the likelihood of misfitting an assignment increases, and bench times may be sustained.

A real-world example of transitioning to IT Consulting 

As a former scientist, the IT landscape can be daunting at first. The reputation of IT is highly technical and requires proficiency in multiple programming languages. The field is constantly evolving at a tremendous pace, which makes it difficult to keep up, even for experienced IT professionals. Scientists often lack direct knowledge of IT systems, frameworks, and methodologies. They are accustomed to slowly developing knowledge and products. Depending on the research field, results can take up to years to discover. Meanwhile, scientists tend to be very thorough in their development before delivering their products. Therefore, a transition to IT consulting can be difficult. The Catch-22 paradoxes and increased clients’ expectations (see sections 2.1 and 2.2) make it even harder to transition and be successful in this highly technical landscape.

However, some academic skills bring great advantages to IT consulting. Most scientists are expert analysts and bring domain knowledge to their field. Scientists possess a curious and critical mind, which helps them grind through large heaps of information. They often have some programming skills and know how to work as a team. These skills emerge when scientists get intrinsically motivated. Therefore, it is vital for such a transition that scientists can rely on their strengths.

The probability of navigating a starting IT consultant to an assignment increases when leveraging these non-traditional strategies:

  • Acquiring industry certifications (e.g. ISTQB, Agile, Microsoft) to tremendously increase foundational knowledge.
  • Working on (smaller) internal projects to gain practical experience while familiarizing oneself with IT workflows.
  • Maximizing internal visibility by working regularly at the office, having conversations with colleagues, and showing up at social/business events (e.g. conferences, workshops).
  • Profiling skills and preferences to improve the chance of landing the most adequate assignment opportunities.
  • Setting ambitious goals to guide you through the transition. This greatly improves the success rate and helps to maintain motivation throughout the process.

Exploiting these strategies culminated in a manager recognizing the potential during the acquisition phase. In the next section, we complement these non-traditional strategies with a business perspective to overcome entry-level market barriers that starting IT consultants experience.

An Effective Approach

The case underscores the power of social networking, proactive visibility, and adaptive learning strategies to overcome the Catch-22 paradoxes. A consultancy firm can aid a starting IT consultant through sponsorship (i.e. an experienced colleague who vouches for a junior’s capabilities). This viable mechanism was suggested by McDonald and Mair (2010). Sponsorship can accelerate a junior's career by reducing the skill gap. Experienced IT consultants should also mentor starting IT consultants and look for opportunities in their networks. Managers should offer guidance to starting IT consultants to acquire technical skills and they should be transparent about the consultancy market. 

Meanwhile, junior consultants should develop formal skills by acquiring industry certifications and practical assignments. It is generally effective to gain expertise in a single field, before expanding knowledge to other fields. Bench times may be prolonged in a tight market, but the time should be spent effectively. Any organization can contribute to a junior consultant’s success by providing practical assignments, social/business events, and educational programs. By doing so, starting consultants may have a better chance of building a career in the consultancy market.
