A quick guide to chaos engineering

by
Yaël Keemink

I would like to challenge you to start with chaos engineering. You should try to break your production environment on purpose. Not a test environment, not a production like environment. No, the real deal.

#Way of Working
#Testing
#Chaos engineering
#DevOps

What is chaos engineering

Alright, don’t run to your server and pull all the plugs, first review what chaos engineering actually means. Some people know the term but only few are actually implementing it in their way of working. Chaos engineering is a term coined by Netflix in 2011. The main goal of chaos engineering is building resilient software by expecting the worst case. 

The Idea is that all systems will eventually break and when they do you want to be prepared for it. You want to know exactly what will happen when a system goes offline. And that is where chaos engineering comes in. It is about something you’ve been told never to do, testing in production. But it is not like any random exploratory test, you actually need to do a lot of work before you can actually pull a plug because you don’t want users to complain about failing systems. With chaos engineering you are not trying to actually break the system, you are trying to prove everything keeps on working as intended with minimal inconvenience for the user.

Integration tests are not sufficient

You might say you have integration tests, unit tests and load tests, so you don’t need chaos tests. However, these tests don’t test your processes, they will not test how the team responds to an actual outage. If they know how they should restore a database or what the response times are. These tests also run on safe testing or staging environments. Which are most likely similar to production but will never be the same. This  means you cannot know for sure how your production environment will react to a certain load or outage of a server.

Before we begin

First of all you need a proper setup. In order to start with chaos engineering you will need good monitoring on your systems, notification on the monitoring, an already resilient system and last but not least an SLA with a corresponding response process. 

Monitoring and notifications

Monitoring is important because without it you won’t notice any deviations in your systems behavior. You want to be monitoring everything you think is relevant in keeping your system alive. Not just CPU usage and if your application is online or not, but also things like response times, queue lengths or maybe I/O speed. This way you can detect issues early on and maybe even prevent systems going down.

But monitoring is not all, you can monitor all you want but without proper notifications you will most probably not be informed about any unusualities. Unless you have someone dedicated to watching these monitors all the time. But that would be a waste of resources. Setup your notifications to notify the whole DevOps team via your most used communications channel when the monitoring finds anything out of the ordinary.

Resilient system

I mentioned you want to do chaos engineering to build resilient applications, yet I also mention you need an already resilient system to even start with chaos engineering. This is not a contradiction, with chaos engineering you will verify your resilient system is actually resilient. It is also a motivator for teams to build resilient systems because they know their system can crash at any given time.

SLA

A service level agreement, SLA for short, or something similar is necessary to know what to expect when something goes down and when you can run your chaos tests. When the SLA states there is only support and uptime guaranteed on workdays, it would be a bit strange if you bring down a server on a Saturday.

Response process

A response process will tell the responsible teams what to do when their application goes offline. With this the time to repair will hopefully be shorter as proposed to just doing whatever. 

Culture

Besides this setup you will need to create the right culture in your organization. No one will want to do tests in production with the risk of breaking it all when there is no safe environment. It is imperative there is no blame culture, there must be room to make mistakes and learn from these mistakes.

Plan for chaos

When all of this has been setup, you can actually start the testing bit. No, don't pull the plugs just yet. We first need to write an hypothesis about what we are going to do and what our expectations are. We need to be as precise as possible in our actions and expectations. 

You want to know if you are going to increase the load and by how much, or if you will shut down a server or maybe even pull the physical plug and from what server. You need to know which systems will be hit by your actions and what you think will happen.

When our hypothesis states we are expecting users to notice the outage we will not proceed. We will first tell the responsible team to make their application more resilient. We will proceed to the testing bit if our hypothesis tells us everything will be alright.

Introduce chaos

Now that we have a green light from our hypothesis we will set a date and time to actually carry out our plan. It is important the responsible team will not be notified. Actually, notify only on a need to know basis. That way they will not be able to prepare for the coming outage. The better you can simulate a real outage the better you can test your processes and systems. 

Evaluation time

After the testing phase you will sit down with the responsible team and review what has happened. What went wrong, what went right and what are the learnings. The most common way to do this is by using a post mortem. 

A post mortem is a template which can be filled in. It usually contains a section with a timeline of everything that has happened including responses and system events, a section where you can write the root cause, a section with learnings and a section with corrective and preventive measures. There are more sections which can be filled out but I won’t go over them all.

The most important thing when writing the post mortem and when discussing the outage (caused by chaos engineering or not) is a blameless approach. You want everyone to be honest and be able to speak out openly. This will not happen when you blame and shame people. This means you will not write down any names in the post mortem but rather write a function title. It is not important who is to blame for the incident, but how you will improve to prevent it the next time.

Automation

Those were all the phases you need to go through to start with chaos engineering, but I have one final piece of advice. Automation is your friend, try to automate as much as possible. The more you have automated, the more you can do things to improve the quality of your application or add functionalities to it. 

Automate your pipelines, tests and eventually your chaos tests. There are multiple automation tools you can use to automate your chaos. This way you really ingrain chaos engineering in the culture.
